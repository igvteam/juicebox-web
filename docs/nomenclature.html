Juicebox Web: a nomenclature

The words in CONTEXT.md, placed on the thing each one names.

Every term below is defined in this repository’s CONTEXT.md, which is the authority. This page adds nothing to it; it only points. Where the glossary lists words to avoid, they are repeated here, because most confusion arrives through a synonym rather than through the term itself.

The mock-up shows three viewer panels, because the vocabulary that gives the most trouble — current browser, target set, aim — is invisible when only one panel is open. Borders and outlines are reproduced at their shipping values.

On the two repositories. This vocabulary is juicebox-web’s. The library it wraps, juicebox.js, keeps a glossary of its own, and the two do not always agree — where they collide, the collision is noted rather than smoothed over.

Where the shell stops

The distinction between shell and viewer is not a figure of speech. It is a line in the DOM. js/app.js calls hic.init(container, config) on #app-container; from that call onward, everything inside that element belongs to the library. The shell owns what surrounds it.

This is why a question like “can we put a badge on the panel?” has an answer before it has a design: not from this repository, it cannot. The panel, its navbar, its borders and its locus box are all drawn by juicebox.js. A shell-owned notice must either live outside the stage, or the library must grow the surface itself.

Practical test. If you would change it by editing a file in this repository, it is the shell. If you would change it by opening a pull request against juicebox.js, it is the viewer. There is no third category.
Shell — this repository
  • The top navigation, and every dropdown hanging off it
  • Modals: the map menu, ENCODE and 4DN tables, URL entry, share, session save
  • AlertSingleton, the one notification surface the shell has
  • The caption strip, the page footer, the entry pages per distribution
Viewer — juicebox.js, inside #app-container
  • Every browser panel: its navbar, borders, locus box, and menu
  • The contact matrix, its color scale, its axes and track lanes
  • The current-browser border and the target-set outline
  • Sync between browsers, and the compatibility rules that govern it

Two groupings, and why they are not one

The mock-up carries two different groupings of panels, and they are the pair most often confused for each other. A target set is what a track load reaches: the user picks it by shift-clicking, and it is drawn as the dashed blue outline. A sync group is what a locus change reaches: nobody picks it, the library derives it from whether two maps are the same assembly carrying the same chromosomes, and it has no outline at all.

Neither implies the other. Two panels can sync without being aimed at together, and two panels can be aimed at together while one of them cannot sync with anything. In the figure, panel 3 is outside both — but for unrelated reasons, and fixing one would not fix the other.

The glossary keeps both terms and lists each under the other’s avoid line, which is the only entry in this repository’s vocabulary where two live terms are mutually excluded. That is deliberate: the error worth guarding against is not using the wrong word, it is believing there is only one thing to name.

Practical consequence. Aiming at a panel does not make it follow. No amount of shift-clicking will sync a dm6 map to an hg38 one, and the panel says so rather than leaving the user to guess.
